"Coming-of-Age"

08|08|2026

Now an average individual would interpret this phrase as a title referring
to the specific growth associated with adolescence including the multitudes of struggles
that come with it; however, the themes these narratives are usually underpinned
by reflect a cycle of life experienced by a much broader age
group — to the extent that it can be argued that it isn’t
age-related at all. I’m confident everybody reading this right now has
read or watched those repetitive — often cringe — storylines of teenagers who are
subject to the atrocities occurring in high school life who somehow change
or grow into more mature versions of themselves. Forgive me for rapidly
diverging, but a connected experience I would like to illuminate is the
audience consuming this media, often want a cognate, cinematic life — but I contend
that it is virtually impossible to have a life resembling that shown
in such media. Firstly, putting aside ordinary students spontaneously breaking into song,
archetypal bullying, and undeniably unrealistic characters — I would like to introduce the
concept I designate as the inflated significance interpreted out of ordinary events
due to the time-bound nature of narratives. Put in simple terms —
which is very much necessary after my preposterous slew of jargon — in
cinema, you often see a freshman who falls deeply in love after
seeing someone give them a pen, or help them carry their books
after it mysteriously falls out of their lockers, and this benevolent action
somehow triggers a perception so distorted to appear significant when pragmatically, a
high school crush isn’t someone you exactly remember on your deathbed. We
often desire such meaningful moments or ‘deep’ dialogues in our own lives
but chances are, you’ve already experienced them, so many of them, in
fact, that most of them disappear into obsolescence. In a 90-minute
movie, giving a pen to someone feels like you have been struck
by destiny itself, but — unless you are exorbitantly delusional — giving a girl
a pen is just a regular act of kindness and unlike a
musical, isn’t a dramatic cue of love. So yes, your life is
a movie, so many movies that you forget the fifth by the
time you’re on the twenty-sixth; can you blame yourself, though? Bridging
back to the actual claim, if you take the examples of the
most quintessential coming-of-age narratives, you would notice that there is
quite a broad pattern: the protagonist is still figuring out who they
are. Take Charlie from The Perks of Being a Wallflower, or Neil
Perry from Dead Poets Society; Neil is battling expectations for the sake
of becoming an actor, and Charlie is overcoming his trauma. Now whilst
this may seem exclusive to puberty, let me remind you, it is
in fact not. Identity isn’t something built once or a progress bar
completed by graduation. It is a constant fluid that has to adapt
to the struggles life throws. Now for coming-of-age to be
exclusive to adolescence, struggle itself would have to come to halt — and
for that, life would have to stop changing. Now this connects back
to the divergent claim, life will change so much that you can’t
keep up at times, that’s the beauty! Now let me ask you,
what is life, if not change? You can be an engineer all
your life and then decide to become a journalist; you can be lonely
till you’re 45 and then you find the love of your life
in a café; you can be a teenage runaway and have a
surprising reconnect with your brother at 32. First, it’s high school, then it’s
college, then it’s your first job, then it’s the breakup with the
one person you thought was the one, then it’s the period of
loneliness and mundanity you don’t know how to overcome,
then it’s pivoting your whole career, then it’s finding the love of
your life, then kids, then sending those kids to college, now tell
me, do you really stop ‘coming-of-age’? Ultimately, it is up
to you to choose whether to fear change, or to embrace it,
and drive down the endless road of becoming yourself.
